The business problem

Hearthstone Kitchens is a fast-casual restaurant group with 140 company-owned locations and about $410M in annual revenue. Every morning, general managers (GMs) write prep sheets by hand from memory and last week's numbers. The result: 6.8 percent of food cost is thrown away (about $9.1M a year), and 4.2 percent of peak dayparts run out of at least one menu item, which costs sales and guest satisfaction. GMs spend about five hours a week on prep planning.

The initiative

SmartPrep forecasts demand for every menu item in every store in 15-minute intervals, converts the forecast into a prep list (how much chicken to marinate, how many batches of rice, by when), and delivers it to a kitchen tablet by 05:00 local time. During the day it watches actual sales and nudges the plan when demand departs from forecast. A plain-language explanation tells the GM why the plan changed ("rain forecast after 5 PM; Friday high school football game nearby").

Constraints and context

  • Team: one product team of seven (four TypeScript engineers, one data scientist, one ML engineer, one QA), plus a shared platform team. No Kubernetes expertise in house.
  • Budget: run cost capped at $18,000 per month at 140 stores; build budget $1.4M over nine months.
  • Cloud: Hearthstone has an existing enterprise agreement with one major cloud provider and a corporate identity provider with SSO and MFA.
  • Stores: consumer-grade broadband; about 2 percent of store-days see an outage of more than an hour. Two tablets per kitchen.
  • Privacy: POS payloads contain customer name, phone, email and partial card data. SmartPrep has no need for any of it.
The architect's first move

Before choosing anything, quantify the load. 150,000 orders a day sounds large. It is about 7 orders per second at the busiest hour, roughly 30 line-item events per second. That single number rules out most of the over-engineered designs an AI tool might propose. See the SAD, section 5.

Why solution architecture exists

Architecture is the set of decisions that are expensive to change later. The solution architect's job is to make those few decisions deliberately, write them down, and keep everything else cheap to change.

The problem it solves

Requirements say what the business needs. Code says how one engineer solved one piece. Between the two sits a gap: how the pieces fit, which qualities matter most, what happens when something fails, what it costs to run, and what we will regret in two years. Left unmanaged, that gap fills with accidental decisions: whichever framework the first developer knew, whichever database the AI assistant suggested.

Cost of change

Changing a database after launch costs 10 to 100 times more than choosing well up front. Architecture concentrates attention on the decisions with that profile.

Shared understanding

A team of seven, a platform team, security and a vendor all need the same mental model. Diagrams and decision records are how a model is shared.

Risk made visible

Performance, security, cost and operability failures are rarely bugs in a line of code. They are properties of the whole, and only a whole-system view catches them.

Why it matters more with AI-generated designs

AI tools now produce a plausible architecture in seconds. Plausible is the problem. Generated designs tend to be over-engineered (microservices and Kubernetes for 30 events per second), under-specified on failure ("the system retries" with no limit or backoff), and silent about cost. Your value as an AI Solution Architect is not typing the design. It is directing the tool with the right constraints, then evaluating the output against measurable criteria and approving only what survives.

The solution architect translates approved requirements into a buildable, operable, affordable design, and owns the trade-offs that design makes.

What you can safely delegate

Class design, naming, framework configuration, test structure, most library choices inside a module. Delegate these to engineers and AI tools; review them only through tests and fitness functions. Your attention is the scarce resource. Spend it on decisions that are expensive to reverse, cross team boundaries, or carry cost, security or compliance consequences.

Rule of thumb

If a decision can be reversed in a day by one engineer, it is not architecture. If reversing it needs a migration, a contract change or a budget conversation, it is.

Functional requirements say what the system does. Quality attributes say how well. Architecture is mostly driven by the second list, and a quality attribute without a number is a wish.

Writing one well: the quality-attribute scenario

Use six parts: source (who or what), stimulus (what happens), environment (under which conditions), artifact (which part), response (what the system does), response measure (the number).

Example from SmartPrep

When store internet (source) drops (stimulus) during the lunch peak (environment), the kitchen app (artifact) continues to show the prep list and record completions and overrides (response) for at least 8 hours, losing zero mutations on reconnect (measure).

Prioritize, because they conflict

Availability fights cost. Consistency fights latency. Security fights convenience. Agree weights with the business before you design, then let them break ties.

Review lens

  • Does every quality attribute have a number, a unit and a measurement method?
  • Is there an agreed priority order, so conflicts have a tie-breaker?
  • Is each target justified by a business consequence, not copied from a template?

Architecture styles: monolith, services, event-driven

A style is the overall shape of the system. Choose it from the load, the team and the rate of change, not from fashion. The most common failure in AI-generated designs is choosing microservices for a problem one well-structured application would handle.

StyleChoose whenCosts you payRed flag in a design
Modular monolithOne or two teams; load fits a few instances; domain still being learnedScales as a unit; one bad deploy affects all modules; needs discipline to keep boundariesNo module boundaries at all ("big ball of mud")
MicroservicesMany teams that must deploy independently; parts with very different scaling or availability needsNetwork failures, distributed data, contract versioning, platform and on-call cost, observability burdenMore services than engineers; shared database across services
Event-drivenBursty producers, many consumers, work that can happen asynchronously, need for audit or replayEventual consistency, harder debugging, duplicate and out-of-order delivery must be handledNo idempotency on consumers; no dead-letter handling
ServerlessSpiky, low or unpredictable load; small stateless functionsCold starts, vendor coupling, cost cliffs at sustained load, local testing difficultyLong-running or stateful work forced into functions

Styles combine

Real systems mix. SmartPrep is a modular monolith with an event-driven ingestion edge: POS webhooks land on a managed queue (absorbing bursts and allowing replay) and one application consumes them. That gets the main benefit of event-driven design where it pays (bursty, external, at-least-once input) without paying for distributed services everywhere.

Justify the style against the load

SmartPrep's peak is about 30 events per second; the design target is 300. A single application instance on modest hardware handles thousands of simple inserts per second. Nothing in the load requires independent scaling of parts. See ADR-001.

Review lens

  • Is the style justified with numbers (load, team size, deploy frequency), not adjectives?
  • Does each service or module have a single owner and its own data?
  • If asynchronous, are duplicates, ordering and dead letters handled?

The C4 model

C4 (Context, Containers, Components, Code) is a set of zoom levels for software diagrams. It replaces boxes-and-arrows guesswork with a small, consistent vocabulary that executives and engineers read the same way.

The vocabulary

  • Person: a human user role (Kitchen crew, General manager).
  • Software system: the thing being built, or an external system it depends on (POS platform).
  • Container: a separately deployable or runnable unit: a web app, an API, a database, a queue. Not a Docker container necessarily.
  • Component: a grouping of related functionality inside a container, behind a clear interface (Prep planning engine).
  • Relationship: a labelled arrow saying what flows and how ("Order webhooks, HTTPS + HMAC").

Rules that make diagrams trustworthy

  1. Every diagram has a title, a scope and a legend.
  2. Every box states its type and technology; every arrow states intent and protocol.
  3. One level per diagram. Do not mix servers and classes in one picture.
  4. Diagrams live with the code and are reviewed like code. A stale diagram is worse than none.

Review lens

  • Can a new engineer explain the system from the context and container diagrams alone?
  • Does every external dependency in the container view appear in the risk register?
  • Are trust boundaries and data containing personal information visible?

Architecture Decision Records

An ADR is a short document that captures one significant decision: the context, the options considered, the choice, and its consequences. Its most valuable section is the one people skip: the rejected options and why.

The template

What counts as significant

Write an ADR when the decision affects structure, a quality attribute, a dependency or a cost line; when it is expensive to reverse; or when someone will later ask "why on earth did we do it this way?" SmartPrep has six. A typical initiative has between five and fifteen.

Common failure in AI-generated ADRs

The rejected options are straw men ("Option B: do nothing"), and the consequences list only benefits. Send it back. An ADR with no negative consequences is a sales pitch.

Trade-off analysis

There are no best architectures, only trade-offs that fit a context. Trade-off analysis makes those trade-offs explicit, weighted and reviewable, so the decision survives the meeting where someone asks "why not X?".

The weighted decision matrix

  1. Pick criteria from the quality attributes, not from a vendor's feature list. Four to seven criteria.
  2. Weight them before scoring (summing to 100), with the business owner in the room. Weights set after scoring are rationalization.
  3. Score each option 1 to 5 per criterion, with a one-line justification for every score.
  4. Compute weighted totals, then sanity-check: would a reasonable change in weights flip the result? If yes, say so.
  5. State the costs of the winner. The matrix picks; the consequences section tells the truth.

Classic tensions to name explicitly

Consistency vs availability

Under network partition, choose which to keep. SmartPrep's tablet chooses availability (works offline) and reconciles later.

Latency vs cost

Pre-computing prep lists overnight is cheap; computing on demand would be fresh but costly and fragile at 05:00.

Flexibility vs simplicity

Every extension point is a cost today for a benefit that may never come. Build the seam, not the framework.

Speed now vs speed later

Shortcuts are fine when logged as decisions with a review trigger; they are debt when nobody knows they exist.

Fitness functions

A fitness function is an automated, objective check that an architectural characteristic still holds. It turns "we designed it to be X" into "the build fails if it stops being X".

Why they matter for AI-built systems

When AI tools write much of the code, architecture erodes quickly: a helpful assistant imports the database client into the UI layer, or adds a synchronous call to the LLM inside the prep engine. Fitness functions catch erosion at the pull request, not at the post-mortem.

Approval rule

Do not approve a quality target that has no fitness function or measurement attached. If it cannot be measured, it will not be maintained.

Build vs buy

Build what differentiates you; buy or rent what does not. The hard part is being honest about which is which, and about the total cost of each.

The decision test

  1. Is it core to how we win? If competitors could buy the same thing and it would not matter, buy.
  2. Does a mature product fit at least 80% of the need without customization? Heavy customization of a bought product is the worst of both worlds.
  3. Compare three-year total cost: licences, integration, operations, people, exit cost. Not just year one.
  4. Assess control and risk: data ownership, explainability, vendor viability, lock-in, roadmap dependence.
  5. Consider the middle path: rent the platform, build the differentiating logic on top.

Good vs bad architecture

Good architecture is not the most sophisticated design. It is the simplest design that meets the measurable quality targets, with its trade-offs written down.

Signs of good architecture

  • Style justified by numbers: load, team size, change rate
  • Every quality attribute has a target and a fitness function
  • Every significant decision has an ADR with real rejected options
  • Failure paths designed first: timeouts, retries, fallbacks, dead letters
  • Idempotent side effects; replay is safe
  • Clear module boundaries with single owners
  • Run cost estimated per unit of business (per store-day)
  • Boring technology wherever boring works

Signs of bad architecture

  • Microservices, Kubernetes and a service mesh for 30 requests per second
  • "Scalable, secure, highly available" with no numbers
  • One option considered, or straw-man alternatives
  • "The system retries" with no limit, backoff or recovery path
  • An LLM producing numbers that drive operations
  • A shared database written by several services
  • No cost section, or cost only for year one
  • New technology chosen because it is interesting

Over-engineering is a defect

Over-engineering is not "extra safety". It costs money every month, slows delivery, adds failure modes and makes the system harder to hand over. The bar: every component must be traceable to a requirement or a quality target. If you cannot name the requirement a component serves, remove it.

A real-feeling example

The first AI-generated draft for SmartPrep proposed nine microservices on Kubernetes, a streaming platform, a feature store product and a vector database. Estimated run cost: $27,400 per month, 52% over the $18,000 cap, for a load of 30 events per second. None of the nine services had a different scaling profile. The vector database served no requirement. It was rejected in review; see Matrix A.

The architect's review lens

Use this checklist every time an AI tool, a vendor or a team hands you a design. It is ordered by the cost of getting it wrong.

Ten questions before you approve

  1. Traceability. Does every component trace to a requirement or a quality target? Remove what does not.
  2. Measurable quality. Does every quality attribute have a number, a unit and a fitness function?
  3. Load-justified style. Is the style justified with the actual peak load, team size and change rate?
  4. Decisions recorded. Is there an ADR for every expensive-to-reverse decision, with real rejected options and negative consequences?
  5. Failure first. For every external call: timeout, capped retries, circuit breaker or fallback, dead letters?
  6. Idempotency. Can every side effect run twice safely? What is the key?
  7. Data and privacy. Is classified data identified, minimized, and bounded? Where is the trust boundary?
  8. AI safety. Does any model output drive an action without a human, a gate or a deterministic check? Can the LLM invent a number?
  9. Cost. Is run cost estimated per unit of business, with the biggest lines named, against the budget?
  10. Operability. Who gets paged, for what, and what do they do? Is there a runbook and a rollback?

Verdicts

VerdictWhen
ApproveAll ten pass; residual risks accepted by a named owner.
Approve with conditionsMinor gaps with dated actions and owners (for example: load test before pilot).
RejectAny of questions 2, 3, 5, 7 or 8 fail, or the cost exceeds budget without an approved exception.

Directing AI to produce designs

The quality of an AI-generated design is set mostly by the brief you give it. Give it constraints, numbers and the shape of the output, then review against the lens.

The brief: six things to always include

  1. The approved requirements, with IDs, so every output can cite them.
  2. The load numbers: peak events per second, data volume, users, growth. Without these the tool defaults to web-scale.
  3. The constraints: team size and skills, budget per month, mandated cloud and identity provider, compliance.
  4. The quality-attribute weights, so it knows what wins a tie.
  5. An explicit simplicity instruction: "Prefer the simplest design that meets the targets. Justify every component against a requirement."
  6. The output contract: which documents, which sections, ADRs with at least two rejected options, trade-offs with costs.

A prompt you can adapt

Design brief prompt

You are a solution architect. Design the architecture for the requirements below (FR-01 to FR-09). Peak load is 30 events per second; design for 300. Team: seven engineers, TypeScript, no Kubernetes experience. Run budget: $18,000 per month. Quality weights: deadline reliability 25, accuracy 20, cost 15, offline resilience 15, security 10, latency 8, evolvability 7. Prefer the simplest design that meets the targets; justify every component against a requirement ID. Produce: a C4 context and container description; an NFR table with measurable targets and a fitness function each; ADRs for every significant decision with at least two genuine rejected options and negative consequences; a run-cost estimate per store-day. Flag anything you assumed.

Follow-up prompts that expose weak designs

  • "Which component would you remove first if the budget were cut by 30%? What would we lose?"
  • "Walk through what happens when the LLM API is down for two hours at 04:00."
  • "Show me every place a duplicate event could cause a duplicate side effect."
  • "Which of your quality targets cannot be measured in our current tooling?"
  • "Argue for the option you rejected in ADR-001 as strongly as you can."

9AI and ML architecture

9.1 Forecasting

  • Model: gradient-boosted trees (one global model across stores with store and item features), predicting p50 and p90 quantiles per item per 15-minute interval.
  • Features: lagged sales (1, 7, 14, 28 days, same interval), day of week, holidays, paydays, weather forecast, local events, promotions, menu-item age.
  • Cadence: retrain weekly; score nightly at 01:30 local per region; intraday scaling every 30 minutes (rules-based, not model re-scoring).
  • Cold start: new stores and new items borrow from a similar-store cohort for 28 days; flagged in the console.
  • Promotion gate: a new model version is promoted only if its WAPE on the 28-day holdout is no worse than the current model in every store cohort, and below 22% overall.
  • Fallback: if a nightly run fails its quality gate, the last good forecast (rolled forward) or a seasonal-naive forecast is used and the store is flagged.

9.2 Prep planning (deterministic)

The prep engine is plain rules code. It explodes item forecasts into component quantities using recipes and yields, applies on-hand stock and par levels, plans to the p50 for long shelf-life items and toward the p90 for short-prep, high-stockout-cost items, rounds to batch sizes, and schedules tasks against shelf-life windows. It is fully unit-tested and reproducible from its inputs.

9.3 Explanations (generative, bounded)

The explanation service sends the top three forecast drivers (as categorical facts such as weather=rain_after_17) to the LLM and asks for two plain sentences. All numbers shown to users come from templates, never from the LLM. Output is checked: numerals are rejected, every claim must map to a supplied driver, and length is capped. On any failure or timeout, a template sentence is shown instead. The LLM is never on the critical path to publishing a prep list.

Design principle

Deterministic core, generative edge. The model decides quantities within a tested pipeline; the LLM only words the reason. A human (the GM) can override any number.

ADR-001

Modular monolith with an event-driven ingestion edge

Accepted
Date 2026-11-04 · Deciders: Solution Architect, Tech Lead, VP Restaurant Technology · Supersedes: none
Context
Peak load is about 30 line-item events per second; the design target is 300. The team is seven people with TypeScript skills and no Kubernetes operations experience. Run cost is capped at $18,000 per month. Prep rules will change weekly during the pilot. An initial AI-generated design proposed nine microservices on Kubernetes.
Options considered
  • A. Modular monolith + managed queue at the ingestion edge (chosen). One deployable with enforced module boundaries; a stateless ingestion worker in front. Weighted score 4.50.
  • B. Microservices on Kubernetes (rejected). Independent deploys and scaling. Rejected: $27,400 per month (52% over cap); requires platform skills the team lacks; no module has a distinct scaling or availability profile; distributed transactions between prep, overrides and audit add failure modes for no benefit. Score 3.05.
  • C. Serverless functions throughout (rejected). Pay-per-use, no servers. Rejected: the nightly prep explosion and sync API are sustained workloads where per-invocation pricing costs more ($17,900 per month); cold starts at 04:45 when 280 tablets sync; local testing of many functions is harder for a small team. Score 3.60.
Decision
We will build SmartPrep as one TypeScript application with nine internal modules, each owning its tables and exposing a public interface, fronted by a stateless ingestion worker and a managed queue.
Consequences
  • Positive: lowest run cost ($14,800 per month); one pipeline, one deploy, one on-call rotation; refactoring across modules is cheap while the domain is learned.
  • Negative: the application scales as a unit; a bad deploy affects all modules (mitigated by blue-green and automated rollback); boundaries erode without enforcement, so an architecture test is mandatory; a future team split would need module extraction work.
Fitness function
Architecture test fails the build on cross-module internal imports. Load test at 300 events/s nightly on staging.
Revisit when
A second team needs an independent release cadence, or a module needs more than 4x the capacity of the rest, or sustained load exceeds 1,000 events/s.
ADR-002

Managed queue between POS webhooks and the application

Accepted
Date 2026-11-04 · Deciders: Solution Architect, Tech Lead, Integration Lead
Context
The POS vendor delivers order webhooks at least once and retries for 24 hours on non-2xx responses. Load is bursty (lunch peak, promotions, retry floods after store outages). Application deploys and database maintenance must not lose orders.
Options considered
  • A. Ingestion worker + managed queue with DLQ (chosen). Worker acknowledges only after durable enqueue; consumers insert idempotently.
  • B. Webhooks direct to the application (rejected). Simplest. Rejected: any application deploy, slow query or database failover turns into POS retries and backlogs; no replay; PII would reach the main application before being stripped.
  • C. Managed streaming platform (rejected). Ordered partitions, long retention, many consumers. Rejected: about $2,100 per month more; one consumer today; ordering is not required because inserts are idempotent and keyed by event ID.
Decision
We will place a managed queue (7-day retention, dead-letter after 5 attempts) between a stateless ingestion worker and the application.
Consequences
  • Positive: deploys and DB failovers do not lose orders; bursts are absorbed; failed events can be replayed from the DLQ.
  • Negative: at-least-once delivery means every consumer must be idempotent; messages may arrive out of order (acceptable: aggregation is by interval); one more managed service to monitor ($400 per month).
Fitness function
Replay test: the same 10,000 events delivered twice produce identical row counts. Ingestion lag p95 under 60 s at 300 events/s.
Revisit when
Three or more independent consumers need the same stream, or ordered processing becomes a requirement.

Accepted
Date 2026-11-06 · Deciders: Solution Architect, Data Science Lead, VP Restaurant Technology, Procurement
Context
Forecasts must be item-level per 15 minutes, explainable to GMs in Hearthstone's terms (recipes, dayparts, local events), and improve from GM overrides. Accuracy target: item-day WAPE at most 22%. The team has one data scientist and one ML engineer.

ADR-004

Accepted
Date 2026-11-06 · Deciders: Solution Architect, Information Security, Data Science Lead
Context

3Top risks in plain language

  • R-02 Cold start. Forecasts will be weakest exactly where GMs are most sceptical: quiet stores and new menu items. We plan cautiously there and let GMs override.
  • R-05 LLM content. The only generated text staff see is a short explanation. It cannot contain numbers and falls back to a template if it fails any check.
  • R-04 Adoption. The best forecast is worthless if ignored. Override rates are a first-class KPI, and reasons feed both training and coaching.
